How We Know the Weather

U.S. Space & Rocket Center, Huntsville, Alabama · 2023–2025

How do you turn atmospheric science into an experience a visitor can understand?

Scale

3,500 sq ft, 54+ displays, a 24 × 14 ft LED wall

My part

Ideation and working sessions, narrative, review materials, content programming and delivery

Worked with

The museum, NASA, NOAA and Baron Weather scientists, and the production team

The challenge

Forecasts rest on instruments most people never see. The exhibit had to be clear to a general audience and accurate enough for the scientists reviewing it. The first concept was also much bigger than what could be built.

The idea

Show how weather detection works, then connect it to real data from where visitors live. The science gets personal once it's about your own sky.

My role

I led ideation and working sessions, contributed to the narrative, and made and presented the client review materials across 17 rounds. I programmed content, managed the content team, and took over content delivery while the producer was out.

The process

The concept shrank to a buildable size and kept its core: how detection works first, then what the data says about where you live.

The reality

About $1.5M overall, with an $800K AV scope. The wall runs an HTML layout through a video processor that crashes at the wrong resolution, so every content block is pinned to its exact pixel area. If a feed drops, only that block goes quiet.

The outcome

Open since 2025, with the AV scope delivered on budget, in a museum that sees about 800,000 visitors a year.

Toyota experience centers

San Antonio, Texas · Georgetown, Kentucky · Plano, Texas · Blue Springs, Mississippi

How do you turn a factory into a story people can explore?

Scale

Four sites, and 18 interactive and media elements at Mississippi alone

My part

Design and development of interactives, touchscreen apps and media, plus coordination across teams

Worked with

Toyota's brand and archive teams, creative designers, engineers and AV integrators

The challenge

A plant is huge and procedural. A visitor center has to make it coherent, and every screen in it is built by a different mix of people.

The idea

Treat the center as one system. At Mississippi, production became a single journey told across seven screens, Corolla heritage got its own kiosk, and the plant's LEED Platinum rating became a touchscreen story in plain language.

My role

I designed and developed interactive experiences, touchscreen applications and media content across the four sites. At Mississippi I developed content and interactions across 18 elements and coordinated content, creative design, engineering and AV.

The process

Layout comes before content. The production journey was modeled in 3D, screens, kiosk and visitor, before anything was built. On the Kentucky marquee I rebuilt the six-screen panel layout in After Effects first, because anything that ignores the gaps between screens looks broken once it's up.

The reality

Kiosks are built to ADA reach ranges and reset themselves with an attract loop. BrightSign players boot straight to content. In San Antonio, six apps and six video channels stay in sync over WebSockets.

The outcome

Installed and running across all four sites.

Ship's Bridge Simulator

Outdoor Adventure Center, Michigan Department of Natural Resources, Detroit · Installed 2026

How do you make a 3D simulation feel like driving a real ship?

Scale

Three bridge-window displays, a navigation and status display, a real wheel and throttle

My part

Scoped it and directed the build

Runs

Unattended, on a public museum floor

The challenge

A boat on a screen is easy. A bridge a kid can drive with nobody watching, where the wheel feels like it steers a ship, is a hardware, software and space problem at once.

The idea

Make the visitor's hands the interface: a real wheel and throttle, three screens angled in like bridge windows, and Great Lakes landmarks ahead.

My role

I scoped it and directed the build, from the sensors in the wheel and throttle to how the boat should feel on the water.

The process

nDisplay renders the right view for each angled screen, so the horizon lines up instead of one picture stretching across three.

The reality

A kid will slam the throttle. The sensor input is smoothed inside the engine, so even that reads as a real command.

The outcome

Installed in 2026 and running unattended at the Outdoor Adventure Center.

How I think

Find the story

Most subjects are too big to show whole. I look for the one idea a visitor should leave with, then build the room around it.

Weather: how detection works, then what the data says about where you live. Toyota Mississippi: a whole plant as one seven-screen journey.

Make it tangible early

People decide faster when they can see it move. I prototype early, in the browser or in the engine, so the conversation is about the real thing.

The Toyota timeline directions were working browser prototypes. The Kentucky marquee was laid out against the real screen gaps before the first edit.

Design for the real world

Exhibits run all day with nobody watching. Budget, upkeep and the staff who run the space shape the design from the start.

The weather wall isolates a dropped feed to one block. Kiosks reset themselves. Museum staff update the Detroit map without a developer. The weather AV scope landed on budget.

Argue for the idea, then commit

I push for what makes an experience memorable. When budget, operations or engineering say no, I commit to the version that can be built and kept running, and protect its core. The weather exhibit's first concept was much larger than the build. Detection first, then your own data: that part survived the cut.

Between the disciplines

Much of my work is translation: the client's goal into a story, the story into an interaction, the interaction into something engineers and fabricators can build. It rarely runs in one direction.

The Decision Layer

An executive walks into a customer experience center to meet an emerging AI capability. Instead of a slideshow about AI, they step into a small business scenario and make the call themselves. The same system can carry another industry, language or running time without rebuilding the room.

An independent concept I made to show how I work. It isn't a client project or affiliated with any company, and every number in it is illustrative.

Audience
Executives and senior operations leaders on a hosted visit, short on time, mixed technical depth
Objective
In about 90 seconds, show where AI helps a team see a problem sooner and where people still decide, then open a conversation about the visitor's own business
Story
Observe, Understand, Decide, Reflect
Space
One large guest display readable from a few meters, a host tablet within reach, standing room for two to six people
System
Runs locally from content packs, with no live data or AI service behind it. Display and console share one state
Operations
The host can jump, reset, switch language or fall back to static frames in one tap. New content is a new pack, not new code
Success
A visitor can say in one sentence what the AI did and what the person decided. A host can run it without a technician
